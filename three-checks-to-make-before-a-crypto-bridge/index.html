Three checks to make before a crypto bridge

Before approving a bridge, check the destination chain, the token contract and the amount after fees; those details show what will actually reach your wallet.

The Chain Times DeskOct 15, 2026 · 22:23 UTC3 min read

Before you approve a crypto bridge, check the destination chain, the token you will receive and the amount left after fees. These details tell you what will arrive and where to find it. A bridge moves assets or messages between networks, which keep separate records and cannot transfer tokens directly.

The transfer can work in different ways. Ethereum.org describes designs that lock tokens on one chain and mint a representation on another, or burn tokens on the first chain and mint them on the second. Some routes instead use available liquidity to swap assets. For more on how those swaps work, see this guide to Biswap’s cross-chain swaps. The route affects what token appears in your wallet and how much reaches it.

Which chain will receive the funds?

Check the destination network in the bridge screen and make sure it matches the network you select in your wallet. A wallet address can look similar across networks, but a balance on one chain does not automatically appear on another. If you choose a network your wallet or intended app does not support, you may not be able to use the funds there.

Also check the destination address. If the bridge lets you send to an address other than your connected wallet, confirm every character before submitting. The source-chain transaction is the first step; the bridge may still need to verify it and complete a second action on the destination chain.

Is the destination token the one you expect?

Check the token name and contract address on the destination chain. A familiar name or ticker alone does not prove that two tokens are the same asset. Bridges may deliver a wrapped token, a representation backed by tokens held elsewhere, rather than the asset’s original version.

Compare the contract address shown by the bridge with the address recognized by the destination wallet or app. Ethereum.org’s explanation of lock-and-mint and burn-and-mint transfers shows why the token form can change between chains. The token’s displayed name may be familiar while its contract is different.

How much should arrive after fees?

Read the estimated amount before you confirm, including any swap, bridge and network fees shown in the interface. The estimate is what the route expects to deliver; the final amount can vary if the route uses a swap and prices move before it completes. A lower advertised fee does not tell the whole story if the route also gives you a worse exchange rate.

Use these three checks before signing:

  • Confirm the source and destination chains.
  • Match the destination token’s contract address.
  • Review the estimated amount after fees and swaps.

After submitting, keep the source transaction ID and check the bridge’s transfer status. If the source transaction completes but the destination transfer remains pending, the two records help you see which step is unfinished. For most users, the better route is the one that clearly names the destination token and shows the expected amount and fees before approval. That is a more useful comparison than speed or a headline fee alone.
