How a Business Should Size a SushiSwap DCA Order

Set a fixed budget first, then choose each SushiSwap DCA trade and its timing around cash needs, fees and the risk of buying through a price move.

The Chain Times DeskOct 27, 2026 · 15:08 UTC3 min read

A business should size a SushiSwap DCA order from a fixed amount it can leave invested, then divide that total across scheduled trades. DCA, or dollar-cost averaging, breaks a purchase into smaller buys over time. It can spread the entry price across several trades, but it cannot ensure a profit or protect the business if the asset falls.

How much should each trade be?

Choose the total budget before setting the trade size. Sushi’s DCA screen asks for the amount to sell, the token to buy, the number of trades under “Over” and the gap between trades under “Every.” Orbs, which powers the feature, describes “Over” as the number of individual trades and “Every” as the estimated time between them.

For example, a firm setting aside $12,000 for a purchase over 24 trades would allocate $500 per trade. That is arithmetic, not a recommended amount: the right total depends on the firm’s cash needs and risk limit. Keep payroll, tax and near-term supplier money outside the order. For a fuller explanation of how sushiswap DCA orders work, see our guide; this article focuses on sizing.

How many trades and how often?

Set the schedule to match the reason for buying. A short schedule completes the purchase sooner but leaves less time between entry prices. A longer one spreads buys further apart, while keeping the budget exposed to price changes for longer. Sushi’s interface lets users set both the trade count and interval, so compare the full schedule with the date the business may need the funds.

Use three checks before choosing the interval:

  • When does the business need the money back?
  • How much of the total budget should each trade use?
  • Could the business accept the full schedule if the price rises or falls?

How should fees affect the order size?

Account for trading and network costs when comparing a few large trades with many small ones. Each completed trade is a separate swap, so splitting an order can mean more executions and more chances to pay network fees. Orbs says its DCA setup considers current gas fees. Check the order’s displayed costs on the selected network before confirming, and leave enough of the source token to cover any costs the interface shows.

Do not make trades so small that costs take up an unreasonable share of each buy. The practical choice is the fewest trades that still gives the business the schedule it wants, with each trade large enough to justify its execution costs.

When does a business DCA order make sense?

It can suit a planned purchase when the business has a fixed budget, a clear time horizon and no need to spend that money during the schedule. Sushi also offers DCA-market orders, which trade at the available market price, and DCA-limit orders, which execute only within a set price limit, according to Orbs. A limit may leave some trades unfilled; a market order may execute at a different price from the previous trade.

For most businesses, the sound starting point is to protect operating cash, cap the total commitment and then set the trade count and interval. If a price move would force the firm to cancel or sell, the planned DCA amount is too large.
